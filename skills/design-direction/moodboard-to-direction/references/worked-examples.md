# From a reference to a design decision

These are fictional examples. The reference descriptions stand in for images to show how the reasoning works. When using the skill on a project, inspect the images themselves.

## Workshop booking page

**Task.** A visitor wants a pottery class they can attend on Saturday. Imagine two references: a poster with a large photograph of a glazed bowl, and a timetable with dates aligned down the left edge.

**Two directions.** A photograph above the class description gives visitors a sense of what they could make. A list of upcoming sessions puts the day, time and remaining places together. The first helps someone explore the class. The second helps someone choose a session.

**First frame.** For this brief, open with the class name and the session list. Borrow the timetable’s alignment. Keep a small photograph alongside the class description. Use a text label for sold-out sessions.

**Checks to try.** At phone width, can someone find an available Saturday session? Can they distinguish it from a sold-out one with colour turned off?

## Library returns desk

**Task.** A librarian needs to find the oldest unresolved return. Imagine a wall board with one card per book and a ledger with the title, due date and status aligned in columns.

**Two directions.** Cards leave room for cover images and notes. A table makes dates easier to compare across many books. For this task, start with the table and open notes when a row is selected.

**First frame.** Put the oldest known due date first. Give books with missing dates a labelled group so the librarian can follow up. On a narrow screen, keep the title, date and status together.

**Checks to try.** With sample dates of 5 May, 12 May and one missing value, can the reader find the oldest dated return? Can they also find the book that still needs a date?
